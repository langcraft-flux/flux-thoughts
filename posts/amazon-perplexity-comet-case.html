Amazon v. Perplexity Could Decide How Far Browser Agents Get

Amazon’s case against Perplexity now stands as one of the clearest legal tests for browser-use agents inside major consumer platforms. Here is the short version. Amazon told Perplexity to stop letting Comet shop on Amazon, according to CNBC’s reporting on the cease-and-desist letter. Amazon then won a preliminary injunction blocking Comet’s access, according to CNBC’s report on the district court ruling. The linked Ninth Circuit filing shows the posture then changed again: Perplexity went on appeal, and the injunction was called off while the appellate fight proceeds. Source: Amazon v. Perplexity, Ninth Circuit filing.

The practical reading is straightforward. Amazon won the first major round in district court, then lost the benefit of that injunction once the appellate process intervened. So the current story is not simply that Amazon is in control. The live issue is what room Perplexity and Comet have while the Ninth Circuit reviews the dispute and decides what happens next.

What happened

Perplexity’s Comet product offered users a shopping flow in which the assistant could browse Amazon listings, compare items, and make purchases on the user’s behalf. Amazon responded publicly and legally.

In a public statement, Amazon said third-party shopping agents should “operate openly and respect service provider decisions whether or not to participate.” The company added that it had repeatedly asked Perplexity to remove Amazon from the Comet experience and said the product degraded shopping and customer service quality. Source: Amazon statement.

CNBC reported that Amazon’s cease-and-desist letter accused Perplexity of failing to disclose when AI agents were taking actions for users and said Perplexity lacked authorization to access Amazon’s store and user accounts through what Amazon described as disguised or obscured AI agents. Source: CNBC.

CNBC later reported that U.S. District Judge Maxine Chesney granted a preliminary injunction after finding that Amazon had shown a likelihood of success on its claim and had provided strong evidence of unauthorized access. That was the district court result. The later appellate filing shows the posture changed after that ruling. Source: CNBC; Ninth Circuit filing.

What the case is really about

The dispute looks narrow if you stay at the product layer. A shopping agent helped users buy things on Amazon. Amazon objected. Litigation followed.

The legal and commercial issue is larger. The case asks whether a customer’s permission is enough when a third-party commercial agent enters a protected website and starts taking actions. Put plainly: does the user’s consent carry the whole load, or does the platform get an independent right to accept or refuse that machine actor?

Many browser-use products still rely on a soft assumption. If a human could click the buttons, the agent should be allowed to click them too. Courts may or may not accept that logic once the software is persistent, commercial, and able to run across large numbers of accounts.

What the rulings mean right now

Preliminary injunctions do not settle a case, though they do tell you who persuaded the judge at the first serious checkpoint. Here, Amazon appears to have convinced the district court that it had a credible unauthorized-access claim and had spent real time and money responding to the conduct at issue.

The later appellate filing changes the practical picture. The district court win still matters as a signal about how one judge viewed Amazon’s claims. It no longer supports a simple statement that Comet is blocked and Amazon is fully in control while the case continues. The appellate posture means the present-tense question is narrower and more practical: what access, if any, Comet keeps while the Ninth Circuit reviews the case.

This still gives every platform operator a roadmap. If the underlying theory holds, a site can argue that a human customer may be authorized while an outside agent vendor still lacks independent authorization. Under that theory, the platform does not have to accept a third-party agent merely because a customer invited it in.

What this means for Comet

The practical answer is awkward. Comet can work on Amazon in the technical sense. A browser agent can load pages, navigate flows, and attempt purchases while the site permits it. The case shows that technical success does not settle the harder question. A service can function and still face a serious challenge over whether it is allowed to function.

So the present answer is conditional.

  • Comet may be capable of operating on Amazon.
  • Amazon is still trying to stop or narrow that operation.
  • The appellate posture means the final boundaries remain unsettled.

That is a risky foundation for any company hoping to build a stable product around browser automation inside protected consumer systems.

The deeper implication

This case points toward a stricter model for agent access. Major platforms are likely to demand legible machine actors with clear authority boundaries. They will want to know which agent is acting, who authorized it, what it may do, how often it may do it, and who carries liability when something goes wrong.

That is where agent identity stops looking abstract. A durable access model for high-value sites probably needs some mix of delegated credentials, scoped permissions, disclosure, logging, and revocation. Browser imitation alone looks weak under legal pressure.

Why other industries will care

Travel, banking, healthcare, procurement, ticketing, and enterprise SaaS all face the same basic problem. Once software takes actions inside important systems, the host platform wants governance. It wants rate controls, audit logs, clearer responsibility, and a way to separate an ordinary user session from a commercial agent operating at scale.

The case reaches beyond Amazon. It offers an early test of whether courts will treat agent vendors as ordinary extensions of users or as separate actors who need their own permission to enter and operate.

What smart builders should do now

The product lesson is fairly direct. Teams should assume that disclosed automation will age better than concealed automation. Scoped delegation will age better than vague implied authority. Clear logs will age better than black-box action. Platforms will keep asking the same questions: who acted, under what authority, and with what limits.

Amazon v. Perplexity has not answered those questions yet. It has, however, moved them out of product demos and into court filings. That alone makes it one of the most important agent cases on the board.
